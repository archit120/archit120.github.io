## AI, Programming, and Learning

I’ve been thinking about how I feel about AI and programming. I don’t think writing code has much of a future, but that doesn’t make me particularly sad. I never really loved writing code. I loved being able to make computers do things.

The first thing I ever built was a stupid C# app that showed different music videos when I pressed different buttons. I loved computers and video games and wanted to figure out how to make my computer do more things. I was addicted even though this didn’t really solve any real problem.

By that measure, I’ve never felt more excited. I went from a small pre-AI personal finance script to a mobile app with a backend server. I’ve never written a mobile app. AI has changed what feels worth building to me. There were things I wanted that I wouldn’t seriously consider building because I knew that they would take weeks or months. Increasingly, when I think something should exist, I just try to make it.

This is very close to what I wanted from programming in the first place. I wrote code long before I understood assembly. I’ve always relied on abstractions I don’t fully grasp, so another layer doesn’t bother me very much.

The part I feel much more conflicted about is learning.

A lot of the happiness I’ve gotten from programming came from being stuck, thinking for a while, and eventually understanding. Even if mentally taxing and slow, I really liked this process. AI changes that relationship. If I want to build something, increasingly I can just build it.

I don’t think this makes learning impossible. I did maths problems where obviously someone already knew the answer, and that was fine because the answer wasn’t the point. It’s just that programming didn’t usually make me choose between those goals.

I’ve learned plenty of things without needing to. I learned about CPU architecture mostly because I just wanted to understand it. So clearly not all of my curiosity came from necessity. I just don’t know how much of it did.

I think this might also explain something I’ve noticed about the things I’m building now. They’re much more ambitious than the things I used to build, but getting them working doesn’t necessarily feel more satisfying. It sometimes feels less satisfying. The thing I made is better, but I barely get any credit.

Being good at building software is part of my identity. I’m proud of it, I like being recognized for it, and it pays well. I’m not attached to being called a programmer, but I am attached to being unusually good at building things, so of course there’s some discomfort in watching that ability become less scarce.

And I really don’t think the answer is to force myself into building the harder way. Up until now programming happened to give me two things at once: the joy of building something I wanted, and the joy of being forced to think and learn until I could build it. AI is giving me much more of the first and removing much of the necessity of the second.

I don’t know which of those two things gave me more joy.
